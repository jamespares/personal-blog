Last year I prepared my Year 4 class for their ISA writing exam using a simple system. Every weekend, each student wrote a full essay in the exact exam format. On Monday they swapped papers and marked their partner's work against the examiners' rubric — the same rubric every week, until they knew it by heart.

I turned the rubric into numbers. After each session, students read out their scores and I put them into a spreadsheet that showed a traffic light: green for safe, red for danger. We ran this for weeks, and the scores climbed. That cohort got the highest results the school had ever seen in that exam.

So I have a dog in this fight. But peer-marking often fails, and it's worth being honest about why it works when it does.

## The lazy-teacher critique

Some people say peer-marking is just teachers being lazy — outsourcing the feedback that students actually come to school for. There's some truth in it. It does save time. But saving the teacher's time isn't the same as wasting the student's, and the research points to something more interesting.

Large meta-analyses find that peer assessment, done properly, improves results (Double, McGrane & Hopfenbeck, 2020; Li et al., 2020). The odd thing is where the benefit sits. It comes less from receiving a mark than from giving one. Judging a peer's essay forces you to diagnose gaps, apply criteria and explain fixes — and that work deepens your own understanding. Nicol, Thomson and Breslin (2014) found that students often learn more from giving feedback than from receiving it. The marker learns by marking.

I saw this in my own classroom. After six weeks of marking the same rubric, my students could recite the exam criteria in their sleep. Their own essays showed it.

## Where it goes wrong

The research is just as clear about how it fails. Marks given by students are only moderately reliable compared to expert judgement, and students consistently mark each other more generously than teachers do (Power & Tanner, 2023). Without clear criteria, marking gets swayed by friendship, rivalry and uneven ability. Students know this, and many feel anxious or sceptical about being graded by classmates (Fleckney, Thompson & Vaz-Serra, 2024). Used for formal grades, peer-marking damages trust and produces unreliable data.

Then there's a sobering statistic from Hattie and Timperley (2007): around 80 per cent of the verbal feedback students receive in class comes from other students, and much of it is wrong. Let peer-marking run without structure and you're simply amplifying the noisiest feedback channel in the room.

## What the evidence says to do

The fixes are well established. Train the markers properly — don't assume they'll pick up the criteria by osmosis. Trained markers are far more accurate, and the learning gains are bigger (van Zundert, Sluijsmans & van Merriënboer, 2010; Li et al., 2020). Use a structured, analytical rubric: Fadillah and Ha (2024) found rubrics improved both scoring accuracy and learning, and reduced students' overconfidence in their own judgements. Keep the stakes low and the purpose formative, so the mark is information rather than verdict. Anonymity can reduce social friction, but the evidence is genuinely mixed (Panadero & Alqassab, 2019), so I treat it as optional.

Looking back, my Year 4 system ticked these boxes almost by accident. It was formative — nothing counted except the exam itself. The rubric was fixed, simple and quantified, which kept misreading to a minimum. The weekly repetition was the training. Even the public scores, which can seem brutal, worked because the numbers were referenced against the criteria, not against each other's feelings: everyone could see what the exam wanted, and the traffic light said where you stood. The trick is to channel that competitive energy respectfully, and to make sure the students in the red zone get clear instructions and enough support to climb out. A league table with no ladder is just cruelty.

So, is peer-marking effective? Yes — on conditions. Formative, not summative. Trained markers, not hopeful ones. A rubric simple enough to apply consistently and quantify honestly. Get those right and the marking session isn't the teacher skiving; it's some of the most productive learning in the week. Get them wrong, and the critics are right.
