Walk into certain well-funded classrooms today and you'll see something that looks like the future: every child on their own device, on their own adaptive pathway, at their own pace, with material tuned precisely to their level. No child bored, no child left behind. It's a compelling picture with a compelling name. Who could be against learning that's *personalised*?

The evidence is real but thinner than the marketing. The most cited study is the RAND Corporation's evaluation of schools in the Gates Foundation's personalised learning initiative. It found students making gains in maths and reading well above comparison students over two years, moving from below to above the national average (Pane et al., 2015). Promising — but the sample was mostly charter schools that had volunteered to pioneer the approach, which flatters the results. RAND's larger follow-up a few years later was much more muted: no statistically significant effects on average (Pane et al., 2017). A familiar pattern in education: a real but modest benefit, inflated by enthusiasm.

But my concern isn't really test scores. It's what school is *for*.

## The atomisation problem

Strip it back. A school does two jobs at once: it transmits knowledge, and it socialises children into a society. Personalised learning optimises the first job by quietly dismantling the second. If every child follows their own pathway at their own pace on their own screen, the shared life of the classroom — the argument, the negotiation, the group task that goes wrong and has to be rescued — evaporates. What's left is twenty-five individuals with headphones on, each in a private bubble.

This matters because the skills that make adults functional are mostly learned through friction with other people. Graceful competition: wanting to win, losing, behaving decently about it. Teamwork with people you didn't choose and wouldn't have chosen. Compromise, patience, holding a shared standard. These aren't extras around the edges of the curriculum; for many children they're the main event. A system that atomises learning risks producing adults who are perfectly catered for and socially dysfunctional — none of them practised at living with others.

The cooperative learning research underlines this. David and Roger Johnson spent decades on it, and their meta-analyses — hundreds of studies — found that cooperation consistently beats competition and individual work, not just on achievement but on the quality of relationships between students (Johnson et al., 1981; Johnson & Johnson, 2009). Children working towards a shared goal learn more *and* like each other more. The social and the academic aren't in tension. Done well, they're the same mechanism.

## A fair hearing for the other side

None of this argues against personalisation itself. Every good teacher differentiates; the alternative — marching thirty children through identical material at identical speed — is its own kind of neglect. Adaptive software is genuinely useful for drilling foundations, for catching the child who has quietly fallen three years behind in maths, for freeing the teacher to work with the humans in front of them. The RAND findings, modest as they are, point somewhere real. And the children who gain most from tailored pacing are often the ones the one-size classroom serves worst.

So the question isn't whether to personalise. It's what to personalise, and what to keep shared.

My tentative rule after a few years of this: personalise the inputs, socialise the experience. Let a child practise times tables on an adaptive app at their own level — that's exactly where the technology shines. But the essay, the project, the experiment, the debate — the things that make school a rehearsal for life — should be done together, in the open, with all the productive awkwardness that involves. School is one of the last places where people of different backgrounds are obliged to cooperate with strangers. A society that loses that will find cohesion much harder to rebuild than a few points of reading growth are to gain.

Personalised learning is a good servant and a poor master. Used as a tool inside a communal classroom, it earns its place. Used as the organising principle of the school, it solves the wrong problem — and creates a lonelier one instead.
