Living in Shanghai, I spend a fair amount of time being the confused foreigner: misreading tones, missing jokes, nodding along in meetings I've only half understood. Xiaolu Guo's *A Concise Chinese-English Dictionary for Lovers* is that experience in mirror image, and reading it as a British person abroad is an oddly humbling exercise.

The novel, shortlisted for the Orange Prize in 2007, follows Zhuang — Z, as she renames herself for English tongues — a young woman from rural China sent to London to learn English. Her parents make shoes, and they hope the language will help the family business. The book is written as her diary, in deliberately broken English that steadily improves as the months pass, so you watch her acquire the language in real time. What she acquires along with it is a clear-eyed view of Britain, and much of what she notices is unflattering precisely because it is so casual.

English itself comes off badly. Z keeps tripping over the language's logical inconsistencies — spelling that has nothing to do with pronunciation, verbs that refuse to behave — and as someone who has both wrestled with Mandarin and taught English, I found her complaints entirely reasonable. Our language is a mess; we've simply stopped noticing.

The scene that stayed with me involves shoes. Z remarks to her English teacher that the teacher's shoes might well have been made by her parents back in China. The observation is offered innocently — proudly, even — and it is met with embarrassment and something close to disgust. Nothing in the statement is insulting. The discomfort lies entirely in what it implies: that the polite Englishwoman's life rests on the labour of people like Z's family, and that acknowledging this out loud is simply not done.

There is a great deal of snobbery in Britain, sadly, and the book catches it better than most home-grown satire. But Guo is thoughtful rather than bitter about it, and that feels right to me. These hierarchies are socially constructed, not chosen, and it's hard to blame individuals too much for inheriting them — particularly older generations, who grew up inside assumptions they never asked for. Seeing your own country from the outside rarely flatters it. It does, however, make you see it.
