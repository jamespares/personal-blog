A few weeks ago I asked my class what they remembered from a unit we'd finished last term. The content had evaporated almost completely. Then one boy said: "I remember you made us do that debate, and I was terrified, and then it was fine." He couldn't recall a single technique we'd drilled. He remembered exactly how the lesson made him feel about himself.

This sent me back to Leo Tolstoy, who has a claim to be the greatest novelist ever to take teaching seriously. Before *War and Peace* made him famous, Tolstoy opened a school for peasant children on his estate at Yasnaya Polyana, near Tula, in 1859. It was a strange institution for its time. Attendance was voluntary; children came and went as they pleased. There was no homework and, remarkably for nineteenth-century Russia, no corporal punishment. Lessons ran as conversations, not lectures. Tolstoy toured Germany, France and England to inspect the best-regarded schools of the day and came home unimpressed by their coercion. He published his thinking in his own pedagogical journal, in essays like "On Popular Education", and returned to the work in the 1870s with his *Azbuka* — an ABC book reprinted for generations of Russian children.

## The humility of a great writer in a small classroom

What I find most instructive isn't Tolstoy's radicalism but his honesty. He recorded his failures in as much detail as his successes. He described the history lesson he thought was his finest hour — a rousing account of Napoleon's invasion of Russia that left the children transfixed — until a visiting German colleague gently pointed out that he hadn't taught history at all. He had taught patriotism, and rather inflamed it. Tolstoy agreed, and published the admission.

He was also an exceptional learner himself. French and German from childhood tutors; English self-taught; Greek picked up as an adult, reportedly in a few months, so he could read Plato in the original. His great-grandson credited him with some command of thirteen languages. Yet this formidably clever man titled one essay: "Who should learn writing of whom: peasant children of us, or we of peasant children?" He meant it. He saw that his pupils' writing about their own lives was often better than anything he could set them to imitate, and he had the humility to say so in print.

## What actually survives

One observation of his has stayed with me. Trying to teach history through story, he found the children remembered almost none of the substance — but a figure like Semiramis lodged in their minds, "not because it cleared up anything, but because it was artistically related". The detail was wrong, in a sense, but the retention was real. What survived was the shape of the thing: the vivid outline, the feeling.

That matches my own short experience uncomfortably well. Students forget what you teach them. Ask any teacher honestly, in September, what their class kept from July. But they remember the gist, the shape — and that residue does help them in life. A student who has forgotten every persuasive technique may keep the sense that an argument has a spine, that evidence matters, that words can be organised to move people. And they remember, with unnerving precision, how you made them feel about their own abilities: whether they left your classroom believing they were the kind of person who could write, or the kind who couldn't.

## A necessary correction

I should be clear about the limits here. I teach in a Shanghai school with exams, parents and a timetable. I can't let children wander in and out of lessons, and I'm not sure I'd want to. Tolstoy was a count teaching forty peasant children on his own estate, accountable to no one. His experiment depended on freedoms no ordinary school has — and even he quietly reintroduced structure when it helped the children's progress. Freedom is easier to offer when you own the estate.

But the deeper lesson survives translation into an ordinary classroom. Teaching is an art, and at its best a sacred human undertaking. The content is the vehicle, not the destination. If most of what I say will be forgotten — and it will be — then the questions worth asking at the end of each week are different. What shape did I leave behind? Do my students believe slightly more in their own abilities than they did in September? Tolstoy's pupils, the children of serfs, walked into his classroom because they wanted to be there. That's still the standard, however far short of it most of us fall.
