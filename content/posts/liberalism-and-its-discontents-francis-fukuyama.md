I read *Liberalism and Its Discontents* on a balcony in Shanghai, looking out at a city that has achieved remarkable prosperity without liberal democracy. It felt like the right place to read Fukuyama. Here was a man famous for declaring the end of history, now admitting that the system he championed was in trouble. The irony was not lost on me, and I suspect it was not lost on him either.

## The Broad Debate

The debate about liberalism has become shrill. On the right, it is blamed for everything from family breakdown to open borders. On the left, it is seen as a smokescreen for capitalism and imperialism—a set of pretty words that obscure the continued exploitation of the poor by the rich. Both critiques contain elements of truth, but both also risk throwing out something valuable because it is imperfect.

## What the Thinkers Say

Fukuyama occupies an interesting position here. Having spent decades as a kind of symbol for Western triumphalism, he is now performing a more difficult role: the defender of a tradition under attack from all sides. He is not blind to liberalism's failures. He acknowledges the atomisation, the inequality, the sense that meritocracy has become a justification for inherited advantage. But he argues that the alternatives—authoritarian nationalism, religious fundamentalism, socialist revolution—are worse.

I often think about this when I listen to podcasts like *The Rest is Politics*. Rory Stewart and Alastair Campbell are, in their different ways, trying to defend a liberal centre that feels increasingly hollow. They are good at describing the threats. They are less good at explaining why the centre should hold, beyond the fact that the extremes are frightening.

## My View

My view is that Fukuyama is right about the diagnosis but uncertain about the cure. Liberalism's great strength—its neutrality, its refusal to impose a single conception of the good life—has also become its weakness. When a society has nothing binding it together beyond individual choice, you get fragmentation. You get the loneliness that leads people to seek meaning in politics, or conspiracy, or radical identity.

As a Buddhist, I am sympathetic to critiques of pure individualism. The self is not as solid as liberalism assumes. We are constituted by our relationships, our communities, our obligations. A political philosophy that ignores this is incomplete. But as someone who grew up in Britain and values freedom of speech, due process, and the peaceful transfer of power, I am also wary of the alternatives. The Chinese model delivers stability and growth, but it does not deliver the freedom to dissent.

## Why This Book Matters

### The Neoliberal Distortion
Fukuyama is careful to distinguish liberalism from neoliberalism. The latter—the ideology of deregulation, privatisation, and market worship—is not the same as the former, which is fundamentally about limiting state power to protect individual dignity. In my local government days, I saw how neoliberal policy often transferred power from elected bodies to unelected markets, without actually improving outcomes. Confusing the two has done enormous damage to liberalism's reputation.

### The Need for a Shared Story
One of Fukuyama's more interesting arguments is that liberalism needs to recover a sense of national identity and shared purpose. Not the aggressive nationalism of the far right, but something more modest: a sense that we are in this together, that citizenship means something. Teaching in an international school in Shanghai, I see how hard this is to build. My students come from a dozen countries. Their shared identity is thin. But it is not impossible.

## What Happens Next?

If you care about the future of liberal democracy—and I think you should:
1. **Read this book as a defence, not a celebration**: Fukuyama is not claiming liberalism is perfect. He is claiming it is repairable.
2. **Separate liberalism from capitalism**: Markets are tools. Liberalism is a set of principles about freedom and dignity. Don't let the failures of one discredit the other.
3. **Build institutions, not just arguments**: Fukuyama is right that ideas matter, but they matter through institutions. Join something. A school board, a local charity, a reading group. The abstract defence of liberalism means nothing if the practice of it disappears.

For readers: *Liberalism and Its Discontents* is a book by a man who has changed his mind before and is humble enough to do it again. That humility is rare, and it is exactly what liberalism needs if it is going to survive.
