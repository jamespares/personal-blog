In my first year of teaching I kept a small shelf of books behind my desk, mostly unread, bought in the hope that proximity would do the work of practice. Jim Burke's *The English Teacher's Companion* is the exception. It sits within arm's reach and gets used.

Burke is a veteran American classroom teacher, and the book reads like it. There is no grand theory of education here, no trademarked framework with an acronym. Instead there are answers to the questions that actually keep a new teacher awake: how do I get thirty teenagers to read a difficult text rather than pretend to? How do I mark a stack of essays before I die? What do I do with the boy who can talk brilliantly about a novel but cannot write a sentence about it?

The strategies are concrete. On reading, he offers structured ways into a text — annotation routines, question stems, techniques for slowing a class down so that struggling readers are not left performing comprehension they do not have. On writing, he is a great believer in rubrics, not as bureaucratic box-ticking but as a way of making the invisible criteria of good writing visible to students before they begin. He is similarly practical about discussion: how to structure it so that the same four voices do not monopolise every lesson. Much of it will strike an experienced teacher as common sense. That is rather the point. Common sense, organised and written down, is exactly what a beginner lacks.

I learnt a lot from this book, and I still do. What I value most is its implicit philosophy: that teaching English is a craft, not a mystery, and that craft can be improved by deliberate, unglamorous means. In a profession fond of reinventing itself every few years with fresh jargon, there is something deeply reassuring about a book that simply tells you what works in a classroom on a wet Tuesday. If you teach English, or are about to, put it on the shelf behind your desk. You will actually open it.
