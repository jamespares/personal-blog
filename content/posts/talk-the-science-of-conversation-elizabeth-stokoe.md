A few weeks ago I caught myself rehearsing a parent-teacher meeting in the shower. Not the content — the actual turns. If she says this about homework, I'll say that. If he pushes back on the reading level, I'll concede this much and hold that line. I felt faintly ridiculous about it until I remembered Elizabeth Stokoe's *Talk: The Science of Conversation*, which argues, with a great deal of evidence, that this is roughly how conversation works anyway.

Stokoe is a conversation analyst, which means she studies recordings of real talk — hostage negotiations, GP appointments, dating agency interviews, mediation sessions — and examines what people actually do, second by second. The results are quietly devastating for the communication-advice industry. Much of what we're taught about body language, mirroring and "building rapport" turns out to be folklore. What matters is the fine detail of turns: the word chosen, the fraction of a pause, who speaks next and how.

The finding that stayed with me is how skilled people steer conversations without anyone noticing. Trained mediators, for instance, get people to agree to things largely through tiny choices of phrasing. Nobody in the room feels manipulated, because nobody is following a script. They have a repertoire, and they deploy it flexibly.

This is why I've stopped feeling odd about preparing for conversations. Rehearsing isn't weird; rigidly following your rehearsal is. The difference matters. A script collapses the moment the other person goes off-piste, and they always go off-piste. Preparation, done properly, is more like a musician learning scales: you practise the moves so that, in the moment, you can improvise. Teachers know this intuitively about lessons — plan thoroughly, then abandon the plan the second the class needs something else. Stokoe's work suggests the same is true of every difficult conversation we have.

It's an unusually practical academic book, and a reassuring one for anyone who has ever scripted a phone call and then felt silly about it. You weren't being weird. You were just being slightly less flexible than you needed to be.
