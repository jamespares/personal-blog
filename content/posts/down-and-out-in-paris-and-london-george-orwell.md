I read *Down and Out in Paris and London* on holiday this spring, expecting to admire it, and I did. Orwell's first book, published in 1933, recounts his time washing dishes in the basement kitchens of Paris hotels and then tramping between casual wards — the "spikes" — of England. The writing is superb: unshowy, exact, quietly funny about hunger. The passage on the sheer, annihilating fatigue of a plongeur's seventeen-hour day should be required reading for anyone who has ever complained about a waitress.

And yet I finished it troubled. Not by the book's honesty, which is real, but by something harder to name — a romanticisation of destitution that runs through it like a watermark. Orwell, an Old Etonian, chose this life, or half-chose it, and the knowledge that he could leave sits beneath every page. The tramps he meets cannot leave. The book never quite looks at that difference directly, and I think it gets something important wrong as a result: it treats poverty as a realm of authenticity, a place where a sensitive man goes to be purified by reality. Destitution, in my limited experience of seeing it up close, purifies nobody. It grinds people down, narrows their choices, and shortens their lives. There is nothing ennobling about a spike or a doss-house, and the people inside them would trade any amount of authenticity for a warm room and a wage.

I hesitate to press this too far, because Orwell is a canonical writer and, on the whole, a force for good in English letters. His reportage made the comfortable look at what they preferred not to see; that mattered, and still does. But the habit he helped establish — the intellectual who goes slumming and returns with a certain glow — has not served the British intellectual tradition well. It drifts, over generations, into a politics that loves the poor in the abstract while finding actual working-class life a bit disappointing: too fond of flags, too suspicious of the state, too keen to look after their own families rather than await rescue.

None of this is a reason not to read the book. It is a reason to read it carefully, enjoying the prose while keeping one eye on the escape route the author never mentions. The tramps deserved a chronicler. They also deserved, and deserve, something better than admiration.
