My Mandarin is coming along, which is to say that taxi drivers no longer wince quite so visibly. Progress in a tonal language is hard to measure from the inside, so I picked up Deborah Fallows's *Dreaming in Chinese* partly for company — the reassurance of reading about someone else's humiliations — and partly because Fallows is a trained linguist who spent three years in Shanghai and Beijing, and I suspected she could explain what was actually happening in my head.

She could. The book helped me understand my own learning process in more colour and detail than any textbook has managed. Take her account of why Mandarin is so unforgiving for English speakers: Chinese gets by on a few hundred syllables where English has thousands, so tones carry the weight that our richer sound inventory spreads out. I had felt this as a vague, daily frustration. Fallows names it, and naming it makes it easier to live with.

Her loveliest chapters are on what the language reveals about the culture. Chinese builds compound words by gluing opposites together — open-close means a light switch, good-bad means quality, much-little means how much. Once you see this, you start noticing it everywhere, and the language stops feeling like a code and starts feeling like a worldview. Equally useful is her explanation that the bluntness Westerners hear in Chinese speech is often the opposite of rudeness: dropping the pleasantries is a way of acknowledging closeness. This has genuinely changed how I hear my colleagues and my students' parents.

I should say what the book is not. It will not teach you characters, and it is a memoir rather than a method. If you want drills, look elsewhere. But if you are somewhere in the long middle of learning Chinese — past the honeymoon, nowhere near fluent, wondering why you bother — it does something more valuable than drills. It reminds you that the struggle is the point, that every tone you mangle is a small lesson in how a fifth of humanity organises the world.

I finished it on the metro, looked up, and found the announcements slightly less opaque than they had been the week before. There are worse endorsements.
