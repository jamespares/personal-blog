Dai Sijie knows his subject from the inside. Born in Chengdu in 1954, he was sent down to rural Sichuan for re-education between 1971 and 1974, and later emigrated to France, where he wrote this novel — in French, in 2000, with the English translation following a year later. That biographical grounding is what saves *Balzac and the Little Chinese Seamstress* from being merely picturesque. The story is slight: two teenage boys from the city, the narrator and his friend Luo, are sent to a remote mountain village known as the Phoenix of the Sky, where they discover a hidden suitcase of banned Western novels and fall, both of them, for the local seamstress. But the telling is lovely, and the strangeness of the premise — Balzac smuggled into a re-education camp — never wears off.

What stayed with me is the ending, which I will not spoil beyond saying that the books do their work. The Cultural Revolution was clearly a catastrophe, and the novel never pretends otherwise; the cruelty and waste of the period are on every page. Yet it is hard not to feel that something quietly hopeful happens here — that literature, of all things, slipped through the net and altered at least one life for the better. The boys set out to educate the seamstress, and succeed rather better than they intended. Whether her newfound ambition leads her somewhere good, the book leaves open, and I found myself hoping it does. There is a lesson in that for a teacher: once you have opened someone's mind, you do not get to choose where it goes.

It is a short, gentle book — perhaps too gentle for the horrors it touches, and some readers have reasonably found it sentimental. But as a portrait of what reading can do to a person, and as a window onto a period I spend my days trying to understand from the safety of a Shanghai classroom, I found it quietly moving. Recommended, particularly if you live in China and want to feel the weight of how much has changed in fifty years.
