Last term I watched a colleague prepare for parents' evening with no record of which students were at risk, no list of which pieces of coursework depended on which, and no clear picture of what "finished" looked like for the term. She's a brilliant teacher — warm, sharp, endlessly patient. But she was working enormously hard to make up for the absence of a system. I recognised the feeling, because I used to sit on the other side of it.

Before teaching, I was a project manager in London local government. Not glamorous work, but it drilled habits into me, backed up by qualifications in PRINCE2 Agile and a postgraduate diploma in leadership and management. When I moved into the classroom, I expected to leave all that behind. Teaching is often described as an art — emotional, deeply human, supposedly the opposite of the cold corporate world. Two years in, I think that's wrong, or at least incomplete. Teaching is an art practised inside a project. The term has a deadline. The exam has a date. The coursework has dependencies. Pretending otherwise just means managing a project while refusing the tools built for the job.

## The tools that actually transfer

Start with risk management, because it's the least intuitive for teachers and the most useful. In local government, every project opened with a risk register: what could go wrong, how likely is it, how bad would it be, what will we do now rather than later. A teacher's version takes ten minutes at the start of term. Which students are likely to disengage? Which unit is always taught too quickly? Which assessment lands in the same week as three others? Writing this down doesn't feel like teaching, but it turns November's crises into September's minor adjustments.

Then dependency analysis — the unglamorous business of working out what must happen before what. Students can't analyse a text they haven't finished reading. They can't revise a skill they haven't been taught. Mapping these chains backwards from the exam date exposes the term's hidden critical path. It tells you which slippages matter and which you can absorb. Most teachers do this intuitively. Writing it down makes it visible — to your department, and to your students.

The "definition of done" sounds like jargon but solves a real problem. When is a piece of coursework finished? When the student says so? When it hits a word count? In project work, done means something agreed in advance and written down. I now agree the definition with classes before big assignments: what a complete draft contains, what "revised" means, what standard earns submission. Marking-time arguments become rarer, because the target was fixed before the work began.

## Rhythm and visibility

Sprints and kanban boards transfer surprisingly well. A fortnightly sprint — a short cycle with a small, visible set of tasks — beats the vague instruction to "go away and study". A simple kanban board (to do, doing, done) on the wall, or a shared spreadsheet, gives a class what it gives a project team: an honest picture of progress that nobody has to chase. Monitoring follows. In local government we tracked a handful of indicators rather than drowning in data. In teaching, a few measures per class — submission rates, quiz averages, attendance at intervention sessions — tell you early whether your plan is working or just being followed.

The Gantt chart deserves a special mention for exam prep. Lay the term out as bars against a calendar — revision, mocks, feedback loops all visible — and an anxious abstract countdown becomes a sequence of concrete steps. Students find it oddly calming. So do teachers.

## Teaching the techniques, not just using them

The last point is the one I care about most. These tools shouldn't stay in the staffroom. Group projects are where students first meet the pathologies of unmanaged work: the free-rider, the duplicated effort, the panicked final night. Give a class a stripped-down kanban board. Ask them to name their dependencies. Make them agree a definition of done before they start. This isn't bureaucracy imposed on children — it's a genuinely useful life skill, taught through a subject they're already studying. Several of my students now plan their extended essays this way without being asked.

None of this makes teaching clinical. The relationships, the reading of the room, the improvised explanation when a class finally clicks — that's still the art, and no framework will do it for you. But art on a shaky scaffold is harder than it needs to be. The corporate world, for all its faults, spent decades learning how to deliver complicated things on deadline with fallible humans. Teachers would be unwise to refuse the inheritance.
