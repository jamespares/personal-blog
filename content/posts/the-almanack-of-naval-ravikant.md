I came to *The Almanack of Naval Ravikant* reluctantly. A colleague in Shanghai recommended it with the kind of enthusiasm that usually makes me suspicious—too many people treat business books like religious texts, underlining passages they will never act upon. But I was on a flight, my Kindle was loaded, and I needed something that wasn't about teaching or China. By the time I landed, I had filled more of the margins than I care to admit.

## The Broad Debate

The debate about Naval falls into two predictable camps. The devotees treat every tweet as scripture, applying his frameworks to everything from their morning routines to their stock portfolios. The critics dismiss him as a wealthy man repackaging Stoicism and basic economics for tech bros who haven't read Marcus Aurelius. Both sides are too certain.

## What the Thinkers Say

If you listen to the business podcast ecosystem, Naval is unavoidable. He has been interviewed by everyone from Joe Rogan to Tim Ferriss, and the same themes recur: compound interest in relationships, the importance of specific knowledge, the leverage of code and media. It is easy to tire of the repetition.

But that repetition is part of the point. Naval is not trying to be original. He is trying to be correct. There is a difference, and it matters. In a culture that rewards novelty over truth, someone who is content to state obvious things well is almost a relief.

## My View

My view is that the Almanack is best read as a collection of provocations rather than a system. Naval doesn't connect his ideas into a coherent philosophy; he drops them like stones into water and lets the ripples overlap. Some of them land. The emphasis on leverage—using labour, capital, code, or media to multiply your output—made me rethink how I spend my time as a teacher. Most of my work is linear. One hour of preparation produces one hour of lesson. What would it look like to build something that scales?

Other ideas I find less useful. The obsession with wealth as a measure of freedom sits uncomfortably with my Buddhist practice. Money removes certain constraints, yes, but it creates others. I have met wealthy people who are no freer than the monks I sat with in Myanmar.

## Why This Book Matters

### The Clarity of the Writing
What struck me most was the prose. Naval writes like someone who has spent years trying to remove every unnecessary word. In my local government days, I read hundreds of strategy documents that said almost nothing in as many pages as possible. Naval does the opposite. He trusts the reader to fill in the gaps. It is a kind of intellectual respect that is rarer than it should be.

### The Honesty About Luck
For all his emphasis on agency and skill, Naval is unusually candid about the role of luck. He admits that being in the right place at the right time—Silicon Valley in the 1990s—mattered as much as anything he did personally. That honesty separates him from the self-help crowd, who need you to believe that outcomes are fully within your control.

## What Happens Next?

If you are considering reading the Almanack:
1. **Read it quickly, then revisit**: The first pass is for the ideas that jump out. The second pass, months later, is for the ones that have proven true in your own life.
2. **Don't build your identity around it**: Naval's frameworks are tools, not doctrines. The moment you start introducing yourself as "a Naval follower," you have missed the point.
3. **Test the specific knowledge idea**: Ask yourself what you know that is hard to teach and hard to automate. If you can't answer, that is valuable information.

For readers: *The Almanack of Naval Ravikant* will not change your life. But it might change a few of your assumptions, which is almost as good.
