What sort of man runs the most controversial company in Silicon Valley? If you picture a hoodied engineer who dropped out of Stanford to disrupt something, Alex Karp will confound you. Michael Steinberger's *The Philosopher in the Valley*, a portrait of the Palantir chief executive and the surveillance giant he leads, is an interesting book largely because its subject is so thoroughly atypical.

Karp holds a law degree from Stanford and a doctorate in philosophy from Frankfurt. He is severely dyslexic, the son of a black mother and a Jewish father, a tai chi practitioner and a fanatical cross-country skier who until recently spent much of the year living out of hotels. Steinberger, a long-time *New York Times Magazine* writer, clearly relishes the eccentricity. My favourite detail concerns the bodyguards. Karp hires them from the Norwegian special forces, apparently the only guards fit enough on skis to keep pace with him and dig him out of a snowstorm if required. It is an absurd image — the billionaire philosopher gliding through the Nordic woods, trailed by commandos — and also, on reflection, a perfectly rational hiring decision.

The line that has stayed with me is about driving. Karp never learnt. Asked why, he replied that he was too poor, and then he was too rich. It is a joke, but a compressed one: a whole American life — biracial, bookish, broke, then suddenly beyond broke — folded into eight words. I have thought about it often in Shanghai, a city where the distance between the delivery driver and the man in the back of the car can be traversed in a single generation, in either direction.

Steinberger does not let the charm do all the work. Palantir's software serves intelligence agencies, militaries and immigration enforcement, and the book takes the surveillance-state questions seriously, even if Karp's own defence — that liberal societies need defenders willing to be unpopular — gets a generous hearing. That seems fair enough to me. The argument deserves to be made by someone, and it is more honestly made by its believers.

Read it for the portrait. Whether you finish it admiring Karp or faintly alarmed by him will say something about you. I am still deciding.
