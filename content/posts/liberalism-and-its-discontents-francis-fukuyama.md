Fukuyama will probably always be the "end of history" man, which is a shame, because his recent work is far more interesting than the caricature. *Liberalism and Its Discontents* (2022) is a short, sharp book — under two hundred pages — and it is the best diagnosis I have read of where America is intellectually, and how it got there.

His argument, in outline: classical liberalism — individual rights, the rule of law, tolerance of disagreement — was a hard-won settlement for governing diverse societies. Over recent decades, both right and left have pushed its principles to self-defeating extremes. The right stretched economic freedom into neoliberalism, hollowing out the state and the communities that depend on it. The left stretched personal autonomy into a kind of sovereign-self progressivism that has drifted, in places, into dogma.

It was the second half of that argument that refreshed me. Fukuyama is no culture warrior; he writes as a liberal defending liberalism from its own excesses. His point about the progressive left is not that its motives are bad — mostly they are generous — but that good intentions are not a defence. When compassion hardens into orthodoxy, and disagreement is treated as harm, a movement becomes the thing it set out to oppose: intolerant, incurious, certain. The road to hell, as they say, is paved with good intentions.

I should add the balance he himself insists on. The book is not a brief for the right. Fukuyama is as severe on neoliberal economics and populist nationalism as he is on progressive excess, and his remedy — moderation, institutional humility, a shared national identity — will satisfy neither tribe. That is rather the point. As someone who would describe himself as a compassionate conservative, I found little here to quarrel with and much to think about.

We live, Fukuyama notes, in societies of deep disagreement, and liberalism remains the least bad system yet devised for managing it. That is a modest claim. After this book, it feels like a radical one.
