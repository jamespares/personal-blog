I read *Breakneck* on a flight from Shanghai to London, and it was a strange experience to be suspended somewhere between the two worlds Dan Wang describes. On one side, the relentless energy of Shenzhen and the entrepreneurial spirit that built the world's factory. On the other, the cautious, regulated environment of Western tech. By the time we landed at Heathrow, I wasn't sure whether I was more impressed or anxious.

## The Broad Debate

The debate about China's technological rise usually splits cleanly down ideological lines. The hawks see every Chinese innovation as a state-backed threat—industrial policy dressed up as progress. The globalists see it as proof that authoritarian capitalism can out-deliver Western liberalism. Both sides treat China as a monolith, as if a billion people could ever move in one direction.

## What the Thinkers Say

You hear a lot of pundits on podcasts like *The Rest is Politics* or *Dragonomics* talk about China's economy in the abstract. They speak about GDP targets and trade deficits as if they were reading weather reports. What they often miss—what Wang captures so well—is the texture of the thing. The late-night factory floors, the local officials gambling their careers on a new industrial zone, the sheer human ambition that drives people to work six-day weeks for years on end.

It reminds me of the "polite bubble" Rory Stewart and Alastair Campbell sometimes inhabit. They are very good at describing what China's rise *means* for Britain, but less interested in what it *feels* like to be inside it.

## My View

I think Wang's greatest achievement is showing that China's tech boom wasn't some masterplan executed by Beijing. It was messy, competitive, and often wasteful. For every Huawei, there were a hundred failed ventures. The state set the direction, but the speed came from millions of ordinary people trying to improve their lot. That distinction matters. If you think China's success is purely top-down, you miss the lesson; if you think it's purely market-driven, you miss the danger.

## Why This Book Matters

### The Realpolitik of Innovation
Reading *Breakneck* while living in Shanghai gave me a strange sort of clarity. I see the scaffolding everywhere—new metro lines, new AI labs, new electric vehicle showrooms. But I also see the cost. The air quality. The stressed parents. The young professionals who haven't had a weekend off in months. Wang doesn't glorify the boom; he documents it. That honesty is refreshing.

### A Mirror for the West
There is a chapter on how Chinese companies iterate faster than their Western counterparts because they are less afraid of failure. Sitting in my classroom, I wonder if we have the same problem in education. Are we so afraid of getting things wrong that we never get things done? In London local government, I saw projects delayed for years by risk-averse committees. Here, they build a subway line in eighteen months.

## What Happens Next?

If you're trying to understand China, start with the ground level:
1. **Read this book before you form a view**: Too many opinions about China are formed from headlines. Wang gives you the depth to argue either side with precision.
2. **Ask what you're willing to sacrifice**: The Chinese model delivers speed, but it extracts a price. Are Western voters willing to accept that trade-off? I doubt it.
3. **Watch the next generation**: The young engineers in Shenzhen are not ideologues. They are pragmatists. Whether they stay or leave will determine where the next wave of innovation happens.

For readers: *Breakneck* is not a book that tells you what to think. It is a book that makes it impossible to keep thinking simplistically. That is the highest compliment I can give it.
