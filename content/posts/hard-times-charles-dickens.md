Teaching in Shanghai, you hear about the gaokao long before you understand it. China's university entrance exam dominates family life with an intensity that has no real British equivalent — years of preparation funnelled into a few days of testing, on which so much depends. Reading *Hard Times* this month, I kept thinking about it.

Dickens opens the novel in a classroom. Thomas Gradgrind, a man of "facts and calculations", instructs the schoolmaster: "Now, what I want is, Facts. Teach these boys and girls nothing but Facts." Everything else — imagination, fancy, wonder — is to be planted out and rooted up. The setting is Coketown, a fictional northern mill town where the Industrial Revolution has produced wealth and ugliness in equal measure: identical streets, identical houses, smoke that blots out the sky. The utilitarian philosophy that fills the factories also fills the schoolroom, and Dickens spends the novel showing what it does to the human beings caught in it. Gradgrind's own children, raised on nothing but facts, come quietly apart.

The parallel with high-stakes standardised testing writes itself, and living in China it's tempting to draw it crudely. I want to be careful here. The gaokao is gruelling, and the costs are real — I've taught students whose entire adolescence was organised around it, and the uniformity it encourages can squeeze out creativity in ways Dickens would recognise. There is a Coketown quality to some of the urban landscape it has helped build: efficient, impressive, aesthetically unlovely. But the exam is also one of the great engines of social mobility in modern history. A village kid with a good score gets into a top university on merit, no interview, no legacy admission, no polished personal statement. Gradgrind's system ground the poor down; the gaokao, whatever its flaws, lifts a fair number of them up.

Dickens's real target wasn't testing anyway. It was the idea that a human being can be fully described by what can be measured. Coketown's tragedy is that its people eventually believe this about themselves. That's a warning for every education system, Britain's included — we have our own Gradgrinds, and our own league tables. The book is a hundred and seventy years old and, unfortunately, still assigned reading for the present tense.
