Working in London local government taught me that good intentions are cheap and delivery is everything. Lee Kuan Yew's second volume of memoirs, *From Third World to First* (2000), is the account of a man who delivered. When Singapore was expelled from Malaysia in 1965, few gave it much chance of survival; within a generation it had one of the highest living standards in Asia. Whatever else one thinks of Lee, that fact is not spin.

What I admire most is the pragmatism. Lee had no patience for ideology in either direction — he borrowed what worked, from the Japanese, the Swiss, the Dutch, and discarded what didn't. His assessment of Western welfare systems is especially astute. Having watched well-meaning benefits calcify into dependency in parts of Britain, I found his argument uncomfortably persuasive: support should strengthen the family and the individual's capacity to stand on their own feet, not replace them. It is close to what I believe — family before the state, and the state's job is to help people take care of themselves and their communities.

But the book should be read with reservations, not reverence. Lee's views on the press — that it exists to support nation-building, and that journalists who "interfere" in domestic politics forfeit their welcome — may suit a small, vulnerable city-state. In a British context they would be intolerable, and rightly so. A press that cannot be rude about its government soon stops being a press. Singapore traded a degree of liberty for order and prosperity, and to his credit Lee is candid that it was a trade. Readers should be equally candid about whether they would accept the price.

Living in Shanghai, I see echoes of the Singaporean and Japanese model everywhere — export discipline, a serious bureaucracy, ferocious investment in education and infrastructure — and I have great respect for what it achieved across East Asia. This memoir is the best single account of how that model was built, written by the man who built it. Read it as a practitioner writing about practice. Just don't mistake competence for a complete theory of the good society.
