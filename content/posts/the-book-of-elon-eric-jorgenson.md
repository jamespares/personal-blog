A confession: I picked up *The Book of Elon* expecting to dislike it. The genre of curated billionaire wisdom usually leaves me cold. But Eric Jorgenson, who did the same job on Naval Ravikant, has spent five years distilling Musk's interviews, transcripts and tweets into something closer to an operating manual than a hagiography — Musk's own words, organised around purpose, work and company-building, with a foreword by Naval himself. It is, I admit, a great read.

Whatever one thinks of the man, the engineering record is genuinely astonishing. Reusable orbital rockets that land themselves; the company that dragged the entire car industry into the electric age. Jorgenson's method — letting the ideas stand in their owner's voice — works best here, because Musk thinks from first principles in public, and watching him reason about battery costs or rocket materials is more instructive than any summary could be.

Where I part company is the transhumanism. The brain–computer ambitions threaded through the later chapters leave me unmoved, and occasionally uneasy. Merging minds with machines strikes me as a solution in search of a problem, and a rather hubristic one — the teacher in me notes that we have not yet worked out how to run the brains we already have with much reliability. Genius in one domain is not a licence in all of them.

The idea I keep turning over, though, is the autonomous future he describes for electric vehicles. Once cars drive themselves and answer to a phone, ownership changes meaning: your car stops being a depreciating object on the driveway and becomes an asset you can rent out while you sleep. Every owner, in effect, a small taxi operator. Whether the regulators and the insurance industry share this enthusiasm remains an open question — and I'd wager they have opinions.

So: read it for the engineering, skim the mind-uploading, and watch the robotaxi question closely. It's the part most likely to arrive in your street first.
