Most of my Chinese friends in Shanghai live comfortably. They studied at good universities, they work in offices, they complain about the price of coffee. *Adrift in the South* was a useful corrective to all that.

Xiao Hai's memoir begins in the mid-2000s, when he was fifteen and his family paid a vocational-school teacher 1,200 yuan to find him a factory job in Shenzhen. What followed was fifteen years drifting between the garment mills and electronics factories of China's southern cities — making iPhones and baby clothes, hand-stitching football shirts, cutting plastic for radios. He resigned from ten factories. Twice he walked out to devote himself entirely to poetry, which eventually led him to a small community of artists living on the outskirts of Beijing. The book, translated by Tony Hao and published by Granta, has been described as a landmark of China's migrant-worker literature — the writing that emerges from the 300 million or so internal migrants who power the world's second-largest economy.

What stayed with me was not the hardship itself, awful as much of it is, but the distance it exposed in my own mental map of this country. The class ladder in China goes much further down than my daily life ever shows me. Next to Xiao Hai, my Chinese friends look almost unimaginably elite. Next to my friends back in the UK, they look perfectly normal — same flats, same anxieties, same weekends. Sitting between those two comparisons is a strange place to stand, and this book made me feel the strangeness of it properly for the first time.

I should add a note of caution against easy moralising. The same industrial machine that ground down Xiao Hai's youth also lifted hundreds of millions of Chinese people out of absolute poverty within a generation, and he writes without self-pity. But a society that produces this much wealth while leaving so many of its builders adrift has a question to answer. So, frankly, does a foreign teacher who benefits from the arrangement.

Read it if you live here. Especially if you live here well.
