I recently read about Wang Jibing, a food delivery rider in Jiangsu who won one of China's highest literary honours, the Lu Xun Literary Prize, for his collection *Low Flight* (低处飞行). Wang is 56. He left school at fourteen because his family couldn't afford to keep him there. He worked on building sites and street stalls, and now he writes poems on scraps of paper between deliveries, based on conversations with more than two hundred fellow riders. The whole collection sits in one couplet: who says spreading your wings must mean flying high? Flying low is still flying.

It's a lovely thought. And it made me ask what teachers are really doing when we talk to students about their futures.

## The polite consensus

Schools, governments and the general mood of modern education all push the same message: aim every student high. University, then law, medicine, finance, engineering. Policy has backed this at the highest level. In 1999, Tony Blair's government set a target of sending 50 per cent of young people to university by 2010. It missed by seven years, but the instinct never went away. More ambition is always better, the thinking goes. A teacher who suggests otherwise is selling their students short.

Part of that defence is true, and I want to admit it. For a bright child from a poor family, an ambitious teacher can be the one adult who shows them the world is bigger than their street. Low expectations are a real hazard. I've met students in Shanghai whose horizons visibly widened because a teacher refused to let them settle.

## The quiet cost

And yet. This push has a quiet cost: it looks down on the work of the people who keep the city running — the delivery riders, the bin men, the drivers. These jobs are essential, and in Shanghai they're mostly done with real diligence. So why is it almost taboo for a teacher to treat them with respect in the classroom, as possible futures? Why must everyone be a lawyer or a doctor, when society would collapse within a fortnight if that came true?

Wang Jibing is a useful answer here. By the standards of the careers fair, his life looks like limited ambition. By any human measure — craft, dignity, contribution — it looks like a good one. He didn't need to fly high to fly.

## Where this leaves me

The two sides pull against each other. The case for pushing ambition is social mobility, and the moral risk of writing children off early. The case for realism is honesty: most people won't become surgeons, someone has to do the essential work, and a society that calls that work failure teaches most of its members to feel like failures. If all work were paid properly and respected properly, it would matter much less where a student ended up — as long as they worked hard, looked after their health and their finances, and treated other people with respect.

My tentative view is that the choice is partly false. Maybe the teacher's job isn't to set the altitude but to teach students to take their own lives seriously — to be ambitious about effort, craft and character, whatever the job. But I'm genuinely torn. Push every student towards the prestigious professions and we quietly degrade the ordinary ones. Counsel realism and we risk upsetting parents — or worse, capping a child's potential before it has had the chance to show itself.

So I'd invite anyone reading this — teachers, parents, policymakers — to tell me what they think. Do we keep pushing lofty ambitions and accept the snobbery that comes with it? Or do we talk honestly with students about the full range of honourable lives, and risk being accused of expecting too little? I'm interested to hear.
