I read *The Old Man and the Sea* in a single sitting on a train, which is the right way to read it. It's barely a hundred pages, and Hemingway clearly intended it to be swallowed whole.

The story is famous enough to summarise in a breath. Santiago, an ageing Cuban fisherman, has gone eighty-four days without a catch. On the eighty-fifth he hooks an enormous marlin, fights it for three days far out in the Gulf Stream, kills it, lashes it to his boat — and then watches the sharks strip it to a skeleton on the journey home. It won the Pulitzer Prize in 1953 and was singled out when Hemingway received the Nobel Prize the following year.

I enjoyed it, and I want to be honest about the texture of that enjoyment. The prose is extraordinarily simple. Short declarative sentences, endless repetition, the same words circling back on themselves — the fish, the line, the hands, the boy. At its best this produces a kind of incantation, and you feel the rhythm of the sea in it. At its worst it tips over into something that feels like a fable that has gone on slightly too long. There were stretches in the middle of the struggle where I found myself thinking: yes, the line is cutting your hand, I understand, the hand was also cut two pages ago.

I say this with respect, because the simplicity is obviously the point, and because stripping language back that far is harder than it looks. Hemingway spent a career learning to leave things out, and the emotional force of the ending — the tourists mistaking the marlin's skeleton for a shark, the old man dreaming of lions — lands precisely because nothing has been over-explained. But I do think the book's reputation flatters it a little. It is a very good long short story wearing the laurels of a great novel.

Worth the afternoon on the train, certainly. Whether it deserves to be the Hemingway everyone reads first, I'm less sure. But perhaps a book about dignity in defeat is the right introduction to a writer who ended so sadly.
