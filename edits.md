Draft:


Teachers often ask students to mark each other's work. This has varying levels of effectiveness in encouraging students to learn. It is an interesting topic. 

Some argue that peer-marking is teachers being lazy. They don’t want to spend the time and effort giving students the professional feedback that students go to school in order to receive. This critique is valid. It certainly saves teachers time. But that does mean peer marking is useless.

Peer feedback can be remarkably effective due to the potent social influence that students have on one another. They care about what each other thinks. They also care about each other and want to help each other, sometimes more than they want to help themselves, since their sense of self and self-care is still nascent. 

The trick is to ensure there is a clear, consistent standard for the work. That all students know and can easily apply to each other’s work. The simpler it is, the lower the risk of misinterpretation or unhelpful corrections. It is especially effective when the scores can be quantified and students' results are published for everyone to see at the end of each task. This can seem brutal. But it works. You just need to ensure that competitive energy is channeled respectfully and those with the lowest scores are given clear instructions and sufficient support to climb out of last place. 

For example, I was preparing my Y4’s for an ISA writing exam. We developed a system where students completed a full essay every weekend for homework, following the exact format expected in the exam. Each week they brought the exam to school, and marked their partner’s work against the same rubric the exam expected them to cover, which we used every week. They knew it inside out. We quantified the rubric. After each marking session I asked students to share their scores and I inputted them into a spreadsheet which converted them into a percentage with an automatic traffic light system so they could see if they were in safe zone or the danger zone in terms of their readiness for the exam. We did this for weeks and I watched the scores increase week by week. They scored the highest in that exam that the school had ever seen. 

Research:


Current pedagogical research highlights a crucial distinction: peer feedback (qualitative commentary) is a powerful learning tool, whereas peer marking (assigning actual grades or scores) is fraught with limitations.
Large-scale meta-analyses confirm that peer assessment generally improves academic performance (Double et al., 2020; Li et al., 2020), but the benefits are driven primarily by the cognitive processes involved in evaluating work and producing feedback, rather than the act of assigning a mark.

Key Findings on Peer Marking
1. The Cognitive Benefit Belongs to the Assessor
Contrary to what might seem intuitive, the student giving feedback often gains more academically than the student receiving it (Nicol et al., 2014; Bores-García et al., 2020). Evaluating a peer’s work requires higher-order cognitive skills—such as diagnosing gaps, applying success criteria, and articulating solutions—which deepens the assessor's domain knowledge and self-regulation.
2. Accuracy and Reliability Issues
Peer-assigned grades tend to show moderate-to-low reliability when compared to expert (teacher) grades:
Overestimation: Students consistently award higher marks to peers than teachers do (Power & Tanner, 2023).
Noise: Without clear criteria, peer grading can be heavily influenced by relational dynamics, social pressure, and varying levels of student competence.
Student Resistance: Students often feel anxious about grading peers or express skepticism about the validity of marks assigned by classmates (Fleckney et al., 2024).
3. Formative vs. Summative Impact
Formative Use (High Impact): When used as an anonymous, low-stakes formative task focused on actionable feedback, peer assessment significantly improves subsequent revisions and learning outcomes (Fleckney et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2023).
Summative Use (Low Impact / Risk): Using peer marks as formal summative grades generally degrades trust, increases student anxiety, and produces unreliable data for the instructor.
What Makes Peer Assessment Effective?
Research shows that the positive effect size of peer assessment depends directly on how the activity is structured:
Explicit Rater Training: Un-trained peer marking yields poor reliability. When teachers explicitly train students on how to apply criteria, the educational benefit and scoring accuracy increase significantly (Li et al., 2020; van Zundert et al., 2010).
Structured Rubrics: Providing analytical rubrics reduces overconfidence, clarifies standards, and narrows the gap between student and teacher scores (Fadillah & Ha, 2024).
Anonymity: Anonymizing the process—especially via digital tools—mitigates social friction, reduces bias, and leads to more objective, honest feedback (Panadero & Alqassab, 2019).

Is personalised learning always a good thing?

Overall point:
Personalised learning is often touted as an obviously good thing and something we should be striving to implement across the education system. There are obviously benefits to this. But there are also serious disadvantages to it that could lead to poor outcomes for society. Personalised learning leads to atomisation, and it prevents students from learning key skills like graceful competition, teamwork etc. This could lead schools to churn out hyper atomised and dysfunctional adults. Personalised learning is great but we need to preserve the social element of school in order to ensure that society remains cohesive. 



How can we split digital vs analogue learning?

AI is playing a huge role in education. Students use it to complete assignments. They are also using it in school. This obviously makes school a lot easier for them and prevents learning things the hard way and thereby having deeply set knowledge that persists for longer in their minds (if you get it fast, it doesn’t last). Research says xyz. I argue that, based on my experience in education so far, the most effective split is to ensure there is absolutely zero AI or technology being used by students in the classroom. All writing should be hand-written. HW should be timed to prevent AI usage (too fast is suspicious). All tools should prevent copy and paste functions. Teachers need to be vigilant and not fall temptation to the easy life of allowing AI usage and then just AI to mark themselves. Students must learn things the hard way. If they want to use AI for research, fine. But they must be exercising their own brains when they are organising their thoughts and their arguments and crafting a piece of writing or a test. Handwritten exams are great, since they show us exactly what students actually know. 

Is it unethical to automate marking with AI?
AI can automate marking. Research estimates this can save up to xyz hours per week. However, teachers often lose their mental grip on the content they are teaching as they outsource their thinking to LLMs. It is crucial that any automating marking is checked by teachers and that students still receive personalised human feedback. Otherwise school becomes useless and the teacher-student bond is broken. AI can be used but it must be reviewed and packed up in a human way to ensure the classroom remains a connected place where the teacher can maintain their intellectual authority.

How can the corporate world help us become better teachers?
Teaching is often considered an art. An emotional, highly human endeavour. Different from the cold and clinical realms of corporations. However, the corporate world can teach us many skills that allow us to become more effective teachers. Before becoming a teacher, I worked as a project manager in London’s local government, during this time I picked up several qualifications in project management (prince2 agile, PGDip in leadership and management). Many project management techniques are useful for the classroom (risk management, dependency analysis, definition of done, monitoring, even sprints and kanban can be extremely helpful, gantt charts for exam preparation and group projects, KPIs). We can also teach these techniques to students to help them manage their own group work and projects.

Should teachers force all their students to be ambitious? 
I recently read about a chinese delivery driver who won a literary award for a poetry collection called ‘low flight’. The idea is that spreading your wings does not need to mean flying high and pursuing lofty careers in law and finance. It can mean living a simple life, working hard, and making a difference for your community. Flying low is still flying. This got me thinking about the roles of teachers in instilling ambition in students. 
Teachers are often enouraged to force their students to have high ambitions for traditionally valued careers like law, medicine etc. This kind of ideology is often validated by government policy. Most notably the policies of UK government tony blair (send everyone to university). But I have often felt this encouragement subtly devalues the work of simple folk doing important work, like delivery drivers, bin men etc. These jobs are critical and should be treated with respect. Why then is it taboo for teachers to discuss these jobs with respect in the classroom? Why must everyone be a lawyer or a doctor, when society would collapse if that became true? Is this a realistic and healthy standard to set for students? Or should teachers work with parents to set realistic career goals for students, based on what their talents are, and what society actually needs? If all work is paid properly, and respected properly, it should not matter where you go after school, so long as you work hard, look after your physical, psychological and financial wellbeing, and treat your fellow citizens with respect. I am not sure where to lay my sword on this matter. Which side to pick - to elevate ambition, or be realistic. But I’d invite anyone reading this to comment and share your thoughts. I’m interested to hear what other teachers and policy makers think - should we encourage loft ambitions, and thereby degrade the idea of simple professions, or should we be realistic with students and risk offending parents or students?
