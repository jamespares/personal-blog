Can you get rich without becoming insufferable? Most books on wealth dodge the question, and most books on happiness dodge money. *The Almanack of Naval Ravikant* is the rare one that takes both seriously.

Eric Jorgenson compiled it in 2020 from a decade of Naval's tweets, podcasts and essays — Naval being the entrepreneur and investor behind AngelList — and released it in a spirit Naval would approve of: the digital edition is free. The book divides neatly in two. The first half is about leverage: specific knowledge, accountability, and the idea that you build wealth by owning a piece of something that works while you sleep, rather than by renting out your hours. The second half is about happiness, treated not as a mood but as a skill — something trainable, like fitness or French.

What makes the almanack unusual, at least to this reader, is how naturally the two halves sit together. Naval has managed a genuine merging of Buddhist equanimity with capitalist leverage, and not as a branding exercise. The argument, as I read it, is that the same habits serve both pursuits: play long-term games with long-term people; free yourself from desires you didn't choose; keep the ego at bay, because the ego is the thing that makes you miserable precisely when you're winning. As a Buddhist, I found the second half familiar in the best way. As someone who has watched ambitious people in London destroy themselves for outcomes they didn't even want, I found the first half quietly devastating.

I should register one reservation. This is still a curated anthology of one man's aphorisms, and aphorisms survive scrutiny less well than arguments. Some of it is easier to say from a position of already being rich.

But the core claim — that you can succeed without losing yourself, and that calm is a competitive advantage rather than a consolation prize — is one I keep testing against my own life, and it keeps holding up. I re-read a few pages most months. That is about the highest praise I own.
