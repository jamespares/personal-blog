I read *Crime and Punishment* over a fortnight of long metro commutes across Shanghai, and I am not sure it is a book for public transport. The murder scene — Raskolnikov, the axe, the pawnbroker Alyona Ivanovna, and then poor Lizaveta walking in at the wrong moment — is genuinely shocking. I had to put the book down. Dostoevsky writes violence the way it actually is: clumsy, panicked, sickening. There is nothing cinematic about it.

The novel, published in 1866, is usually described as Dostoevsky's demolition of the utilitarianism then fashionable among Russian radicals. Raskolnikov, a destitute former student, reasons that an extraordinary man — a Napoleon — may step over the moral law if the arithmetic justifies it. Kill one worthless old woman, use her money to do good. The theory is elegant. The man who holds it disintegrates.

Here is my honest reservation: I am not convinced the novel refutes utilitarianism as cleanly as Dostoevsky supposedly intended. He never defeats the argument on its own terms. Instead he shows something arguably more disturbing — that the argument hardly matters, because no actual human being can live inside it. Raskolnikov is not argued out of his theory; he is haunted out of it. His punishment arrives long before Siberia, in the fevers, the paranoia, the inability to look at his mother. That is a psychological demonstration rather than a logical one, and a committed utilitarian could simply reply that Raskolnikov picked the wrong case.

But perhaps that is the deeper point. Morality, the novel suggests, is not a theorem you can re-derive from first principles in your garret. It is a shared code — older than any of us, carried in conscience, habit, and the faces of other people — and you discard it at a cost you cannot calculate in advance. Living in a country with a very different moral vocabulary from Britain's has made me more, not less, aware of how much we depend on those shared codes.

Whatever its success as philosophy, as a portrait of what happens when a man tries to live without one, the book is unmatched.
