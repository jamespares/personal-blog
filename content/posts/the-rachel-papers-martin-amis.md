There is a particular discomfort in reading a novel about a nineteen-year-old and recognising, sentence by sentence, thoughts you once mistook for original. *The Rachel Papers* (1973), Martin Amis's debut, produced that discomfort in me on roughly every third page.

Charles Highway is not likeable. He is selfish, calculating, judgemental, and — though he would be the last to see it — extremely naive. He keeps dossiers on his own seduction of Rachel, treats other people as material, and narrates it all in prose of appalling brilliance. Amis was twenty-four when the book came out; it won the Somerset Maugham Award, and deserved to. The writing is superb — every sentence turned, weighted, and funnier than it has any right to be.

What unsettled me is how much of Highway I recognised. Not the behaviour, I hope, but the mental habits: the constant self-monitoring, the cynical little cost-benefit analyses of friendships, the quiet conviction that everyone else is slightly dim. I thought things at seventeen or eighteen that I would now be ashamed to say aloud, and Amis has written them down with total fidelity. That, I think, is the real achievement of the book. It is not a satire of a monster. It is an embarrassingly accurate record of a certain kind of clever, callow young man — the kind who has read enough to be dangerous and experienced nothing.

Amis admitted the conceited teenager reflected his own youth, and the honesty shows. Highway is never excused. The novel lets him be impressive and pitiable at once, which is exactly what teenage arrogance is.

I would recommend it to anyone who teaches teenagers, as I do, and to anyone who has ever been one. You will laugh, and then, if you are honest, wince. The wince is the point.
