A few weeks ago I asked my class what they remembered from a unit we had finished the previous term. The content had evaporated almost completely. But one boy, after a pause, said: "I remember you made us do that debate, and I was terrified, and then it was fine." He could not recall a single technique we had drilled. He remembered precisely how the lesson had made him feel about himself.

This sent me back to Leo Tolstoy, who has a claim to be the most serious novelist ever to have taken teaching seriously. Before *War and Peace* made him famous, Tolstoy opened a school for peasant children on his estate at Yasnaya Polyana, near Tula, in 1859. It was a genuinely strange institution for its time. Attendance was voluntary; children could come and go as they pleased. There was no homework and, remarkably for nineteenth-century Russia, no corporal punishment. Lessons ran as conversations rather than lectures. Tolstoy travelled through Germany, France and England to inspect the best-regarded schools of the day, and came home largely unimpressed by what he saw as their coercion. He set down his thinking in a pedagogical journal he published himself, in essays such as "On Popular Education", and returned to the work in the 1870s when he produced his *Azbuka*, an ABC book that was reprinted for generations of Russian schoolchildren.

## The humility of a great writer in a small classroom

What I find most instructive is not Tolstoy's radicalism but his honesty. He recorded his failures in as much detail as his successes. He described, for instance, the history lesson he thought was his finest hour — a rousing account of Napoleon's invasion of Russia that had the children transfixed — until a visiting German colleague gently pointed out that he had not taught history at all. He had taught patriotism, and rather inflamed it. Tolstoy agreed, and published the admission.

He was also, by any measure, an exceptional student of things himself. His French and German came from childhood tutors; English he taught himself; Greek he famously picked up as an adult — reportedly within a few months — so that he could read Plato in the original. His great-grandson has credited him with some command of thirteen languages. Yet here was this formidably clever man asking, in the title of one essay, "Who should learn writing of whom: peasant children of us, or we of peasant children?" He meant it. He noticed that his pupils' writing about their own lives was often better than anything he could set them to imitate, and he had the humility to say so in print.

## What actually survives

There is one observation of Tolstoy's that has stayed with me. Trying to teach history through story, he found that the children remembered almost nothing of the substance — but a figure like Semiramis lodged in their minds, "not because it cleared up anything, but because it was artistically related". The detail was wrong, in a sense, but the retention was real. What survived was the shape of the thing: the vivid outline, the feeling of it.

This matches my own short experience uncomfortably well. Students forget what you teach them. Ask any teacher honestly, in September, what their class retained from July. But they remember the gist of things, the shape of things — and that residue does still help them in life. A student who has forgotten every persuasive technique may retain the sense that an argument has a spine, that evidence matters, that words can be organised to move people. And they remember, with unnerving precision, how you made them feel about their own capabilities: whether they left your classroom believing they were the sort of person who could write, or the sort who could not.

## A necessary correction

I should be clear about the limits of the comparison. I teach in a Shanghai school with exams, parents, and a timetable. I cannot let children wander in and out of lessons, and I am not persuaded I would want to. Tolstoy was a count teaching forty peasant children on his own estate, accountable to no one; his experiment depended on freedoms that no ordinary school possesses, and even he quietly reintroduced structure when it suited the children's progress. Freedom, it turns out, is easier to offer when you own the estate.

But the deeper lesson survives the translation into an ordinary classroom. Teaching is an art, and at its best a sacred human undertaking. The content is the vehicle, not the destination. If I know that most of what I say will be forgotten — and it will — then the questions worth asking at the end of each week are different ones. What shape did I leave behind? Did my students leave believing slightly more in their own capabilities than they did in September? Tolstoy's pupils, the children of serfs, walked into his classroom because they wanted to be there. That remains the standard, however far short of it most of us fall.
