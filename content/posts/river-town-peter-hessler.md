I first read *River Town* in a Somerset garden, years before I had any intention of moving to China. I remember thinking it was beautifully written but slightly distant—an American observing a world he could never truly enter. Then I moved to Shanghai, and I re-read it. The distance I had sensed suddenly felt like the whole point. Hessler wasn't pretending to be Chinese; he was doing something harder. He was paying close attention.

## The Broad Debate

The debate about Westerners writing about China is surprisingly aggressive. On one side, you have the purists who believe only Chinese voices should tell Chinese stories. On the other, you have the old-school foreign correspondents who believe objectivity is a matter of professional technique, not cultural origin. Both camps are, in their own ways, claiming ownership of the truth.

## What the Thinkers Say

If you listen to the China discourse on podcasts like *Oxford China Policy Lab* or read the more academic corners of the *Financial Times*, you get a lot of anxiety about "perspective." Who has the right to speak? Who is merely performing expertise? It is a valid concern, but it can become a kind of intellectual gatekeeping that stops good writing before it starts.

Hessler doesn't perform expertise. He performs patience. He sits in a classroom in Fuling, a town I had never heard of before this book, and he watches. He lets the contradictions breathe. That is rarer than it should be.

## My View

My view is that *River Town* succeeds because Hessler knows exactly what he is: a guest. He doesn't try to resolve China's contradictions. He records them. The result is a portrait of a place in transition that feels more honest than most political analysis I have read. When I walk through the older parts of Shanghai now, I find myself noticing things—signs, gestures, silences—that I might have missed if Hessler hadn't taught me to look.

## Why This Book Still Matters

### The Classroom as a Lens
Hessler was a teacher, and that matters. The classroom is one of the few places where a foreigner and a local meet on roughly equal ground. You are both trying to make something understood. I have felt this myself, standing in front of a class of Chinese students, trying to explain British irony. The misunderstandings are the material. The failures are the story.

### The Pace of Change
Fuling in the late 1990s is barely recognisable today. The Three Gorges Dam has reshaped the landscape. The economy has reshaped everything else. But Hessler's observations about how people adapt—how they mourn and celebrate and argue in the middle of upheaval—feel timeless. It reminds me of something I noticed in London local government: the policy changes every year, but the human reactions are remarkably consistent.

## What Happens Next?

If you're interested in China, or in teaching, or simply in how to write about a place that isn't yours:
1. **Read it slowly**: This is not a book to rush. The power is in the accumulation of small details.
2. **Notice your own reactions**: Every reader brings their own assumptions about China. Hessler will quietly expose them.
3. **Visit a place you've never heard of**: Fuling was obscure even to most Chinese people. The real stories are rarely in the capital cities.

For readers: I am not sure an outsider can ever fully know China. But after reading *River Town*, I am convinced that an outsider can, with enough humility and time, see it clearly. That is enough.
