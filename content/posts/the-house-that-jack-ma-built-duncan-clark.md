As a teacher, I have a soft spot for Jack Ma, who began his career as an English teacher in Hangzhou on about twelve dollars a month. Duncan Clark's *Alibaba: The House That Jack Ma Built* tells the story of how that man — failed exams, rejected from dozens of jobs including, famously, KFC — built one of the largest companies on earth. I finished it with one question nagging at me, and it is a credit to Clark that he mostly answers it: how did someone with no technical background and no connections manage to build the relationships and access the capital that Alibaba required?

Clark knew Ma personally, having met him in 1999 in the small Hangzhou flat where the company began, so the account has texture rather than hagiography. The answer to my question, as far as I can piece it together, has three parts. First, timing: Ma saw the internet before almost anyone in China, and being early counts for more than being brilliant. Second, trust. Ma could not code, so his entire career rested on persuading people — co-founders, employees, investors — to believe in something they could not yet see. He was, by all accounts, extraordinarily good at this. Goldman Sachs put money in during 1999; Masayoshi Son of SoftBank committed twenty million dollars in 2000, after a meeting legendarily short on spreadsheets. Third, language. Ma's English, self-taught by guiding tourists around Hangzhou, let him talk to Silicon Valley and Wall Street directly, without interpreters or intermediaries. His lack of technical knowledge was in a strange way the point: he sold a vision, and left the engineering to engineers.

I would caution against reading this as a fairy tale, and Clark does not. Alibaba thrived partly because China's markets were closed to foreign rivals, and Ma's later years — the suspended Ant Group float, his long absence from public view — show how conditional success remains when the state is your landlord. Charisma opens doors. It does not own the building.

Still, the core lesson stands and I find it quietly encouraging as a teacher: relationships and narrative are forms of capital. Ma had no money and no code. He had people who believed him. It turns out that was enough to start with.
