Every August, before the students arrive, the same question returns: how much of this year should I plan now? In my first year I planned almost nothing beyond the next fortnight, and spent the year feeling hunted. In my second I planned everything, and spent the year watching my beautiful schedule collide with reality. The two curricula I teach pull in opposite directions here, and the tension is instructive.

The IB leans one way. Its philosophy is inquiry-driven: the student's questions are supposed to matter, the teacher facilitates rather than delivers, and a rigid twelve-week plan sits awkwardly in a classroom meant to follow genuine curiosity. IGCSE pulls the other way. There's a syllabus, an exam board, a fixed body of content, and a date in May when a child sits a paper covering all of it. Nobody at Cambridge asks whether the class's inquiry happened to reach quotation analysis by exam day.

Both have a point, and both fail in their own way. Pure week-by-week planning is responsive but incoherent: lessons become disconnected events, and you find the gaps in coverage when it's too late to fill them. Pure advance planning is coherent but brittle: the plan was written for a class that existed in August, not the one that turned up, and it can't absorb the discovery that half the class never understood paragraph structure.

Read fairly, the research backs both instincts. Wiggins and McTighe's *Understanding by Design* argued for "backward design" two decades ago: start with the results you want, decide what evidence would show students have got there, then plan the learning. It's the strongest case for long-term planning — not planning every lesson, but fixing the destination and the checkpoints before the journey starts. Meanwhile Black and Wiliam's landmark 1998 review found strong evidence that teaching which *adjusts* in response to student learning produces much better outcomes. Adjusting requires slack. You can't respond to what the class shows you on Tuesday if every Tuesday until December is spoken for.

## The case for planned flexibility

My working answer: plan the year in full, but build in roughly 30 per cent flexibility as a deliberate feature, not an admission of failure. In practice, the long-term plan fixes three things and leaves the rest open.

First, the destination is fixed: the assessments, the exam dates, the final products. That's Wiggins and McTighe's first stage, and it's non-negotiable — students deserve to know where they're heading. Second, the sequence of units is fixed, because skills build on each other and the order matters. Third, the major checkpoints are fixed: the moments where I gather evidence on whether the class is learning, not just being taught.

Everything else is held lightly. Within each unit, about a third of the lessons exist only as outlines, to be designed properly the week before, in light of what the checkpoints showed. If the class is flying, those lessons extend and deepen. If they're stuck, those lessons reteach and practise. This is where the IB instinct earns its place inside an IGCSE structure: responsiveness isn't the opposite of planning — it's what the planning is *for*. A plan that can't absorb the discovery that students misunderstood something isn't a plan. It's a fantasy with dates on it.

The 30 per cent isn't sacred. It's a discipline, and the number matters less than the habit of reserving capacity. Under exam pressure, teachers let flexibility shrink to zero and fill every lesson with content delivery — which is exactly when the plan stops serving the students and starts serving itself.

A teacher I respect once told me planning is like packing for a long walk: fix the route and the checkpoints, but leave room in the bag for what you find on the way. I'd put it less picturesquely. A plan is a hypothesis about how this class will learn, and hypotheses exist to be revised against evidence. Write the plan. Keep a third of it soft. Then pay attention.
