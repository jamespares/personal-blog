I read *Clear Thinking* during a half-term break in Somerset, away from the noise of Shanghai and the constant low-grade chaos of a school term. I had intended to read fiction. Something about the title drew me in—probably the promise of order, which is what every teacher craves after six weeks of managing thirty personalities at once. What I found was less a manual than a mirror. Parrish doesn't just describe clear thinking; he shows you, uncomfortably clearly, how rarely you practise it.

## The Broad Debate

The debate about decision-making books is usually framed as a conflict between intuition and analysis. One camp—your Malcolm Gladwell readers—swears by gut instinct, thin-slicing, and the wisdom of the unconscious. The other camp—your Daniel Kahneman devotees—insists that human intuition is systematically flawed and that only disciplined analysis can save us. Both sell a lot of books. Both oversimplify.

## What the Thinkers Say

The "polite bubble" of business and policy thinking often treats clear thinking as a kind of personality trait. You either have it or you don't. Rory Stewart, in his writing about decision-making in government, comes close to this. He describes the civil service as a place where good people are trapped in bad systems, implying that clarity is mostly a matter of getting the right individuals into the right rooms.

Parrish takes a different line. He believes clear thinking is a skill that can be trained, like a muscle. That sounds like self-help optimism, but the book is actually quite rigorous. He focuses on the defaults—the emotional, ego-driven, social patterns that hijack our cognition before we even realise we are thinking.

## My View

My view is that Parrish's most useful contribution is the concept of "defaults." We like to believe we make decisions deliberately, but most of the time we are running on autopilot. The angry email fired in haste. The meeting where no one challenges the boss. The lesson plan reused for the fifth year because it is easier than redesigning. These are not failures of intelligence. They are failures of process.

Living in China has made this even clearer to me. The pace of life here is relentless, and the default mode is reaction. Someone sends a WeChat message; you reply immediately. A parent complains; you apologise and adjust. Parrish would call this "urgency hijacking"—the tendency to let the loudest input determine your attention. Breaking that pattern requires structure, not willpower.

## Why This Book Matters

### The Ego Trap
Parrish is excellent on how ego distorts judgment. The need to be right. The fear of looking stupid. The tendency to defend a position because it is yours, not because it is correct. I see this in myself constantly. In local government, I watched meetings drag on for hours because no one was willing to abandon a proposal they had already publicly supported. The cost of ego was measured in wasted time and worse policy.

### The Power of Rules
One of the most practical sections is about "decision rules"—pre-commitments you make when you are calm that govern your behaviour when you are not. I have started using a version of this in my teaching. Before the term starts, I decide which behaviours I will always challenge and which I will let go. Without that clarity, I react inconsistently, and the students suffer for it.

## What Happens Next?

If you want to think more clearly—and I believe most of us could:
1. **Start with the defaults**: You cannot fix your thinking until you know what is broken. Parrish's list of cognitive defaults is a good diagnostic.
2. **Build rules before you need them**: The moment of crisis is the wrong time to decide how you will behave. Write your rules when you are calm.
3. **Accept that you will still fail**: Clear thinking is not a state you achieve. It is a practice you maintain. Expect to slip.

For readers: *Clear Thinking* is not a comfortable read. It will make you aware of how often you are on autopilot. But awareness is the first step toward change, and change—real, sustained change in how you make decisions—is worth the discomfort.
