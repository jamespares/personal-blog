I spent some formative years in France, so I was always going to be charmed by the opening chapters of Ezra Vogel's biography, in which a sixteen-year-old Deng Xiaoping arrives in Marseilles in 1920 as part of the work-study programme, scrabbles for factory work, and falls in with a group of young Chinese radicals that included Zhou Enlai. The partnership forged in those cramped Parisian lodgings lasted half a century and, in a real sense, held China together through its worst decades.

Vogel's book, published in 2011 after a decade of research, is enormous and occasionally exhausting, but it earns its length. Its central subject is a puzzle: how a man purged three times, whose son was paralysed by Red Guards, emerged to dismantle the very system that had done it — and did so without tearing the country apart.

Deng is by some distance my favourite Chinese leader, and this book explains why. He was a pragmatist in an age of zealots. Mao's radicalism had genuine achievements to its name — national unification, basic literacy, a foundation of industry — but its campaigns also inflicted terrible, avoidable damage on the health of Chinese society, and Deng had seen the costs at close quarters. His famous line about the cat was actually a Sichuan proverb he quoted in 1962 while arguing for reviving agriculture: it doesn't matter if the cat is black or white, so long as it catches mice. In context it was almost a throwaway remark. Out of context it became the most elegant rebuke to ideological purity ever coined.

I confess I hear that line in my head often these days, usually while reading about the radical identity politics that has captured parts of Western public life — arguments where correctness of category trumps usefulness of outcome. Deng's question was always simpler: does it work? Who does it feed?

Vogel is measured where a lesser biographer would have been either hagiographic or prosecutorial, particularly on 1989. The result is a portrait of transformation with all its moral weight left on the scales. Essential reading for anyone trying to understand the China I now teach in.
