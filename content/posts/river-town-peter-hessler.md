I came to *River Town* as a teacher of English in China, which is roughly the position Hessler was in when he wrote it — except that he arrived in 1996 as a Peace Corps volunteer, posted to Fuling, a small, foggy city at the junction of the Wu and Yangtze rivers that almost no foreigner had visited in decades. The book, published in 2001, is his account of two years teaching English literature at the local teachers' college. I read it partly for pleasure and partly, I admit, for professional development, and it delivered generously on both.

On the teaching side, Hessler is quietly superb. He describes the strange intimacy of the foreign-language classroom, where students will write things in English essays that they would never say aloud — about family, politics, the lives they expect to lead. He notices how his students' minds have been trained: the rote habits, the reflexive formulas, and then, underneath them, flashes of real originality that surface when he gives them Shakespeare and asks them to perform it. I recognised more of my own classroom in those pages than in most books actually written about pedagogy. His central discovery is one I keep relearning: the job is not to deliver content but to build enough trust that students will risk thinking in front of you.

On China, the book taught me to watch rather than conclude. Fuling in the late 1990s was a place mid-transformation — the Three Gorges Dam was rising downstream, the economy was lurching awake — and Hessler records the upheaval through small, patient details rather than grand judgements. He is honest about his own foreignness throughout; he never claims to understand more than he does. That restraint is rarer than it should be in expat writing, and it is a good discipline for a teacher too.

Fuling today is unrecognisable from the book, which somehow makes it more worth reading, not less. If you teach English in China, or are thinking about it, start here. If you simply want to understand how ordinary Chinese people argued, joked and endured through extraordinary change, start here as well. I learnt a great deal from it, and I expect to read it again.
