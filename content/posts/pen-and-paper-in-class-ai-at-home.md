Every teacher knows the genre now. A student who can barely write a coherent paragraph in class hands in homework that reads like a consultancy briefing: flawless grammar, perfect five-paragraph structure, vocabulary the child has never used out loud. AI has made school easier for students. In doing so, it has broken something old and important — the link between effort and learning.

The science here isn't new. Robert Bjork spent decades showing what he called "desirable difficulties": conditions that slow learning down in the short term but make it deeper and longer-lasting (Bjork, 1994). Retrieval, generation, effortful reconstruction — the struggle isn't an obstacle to learning, it's the mechanism. The reverse is also true, and every teacher knows it: if you get it fast, it doesn't last. A student who gets a polished essay from a chatbot in thirty seconds has acquired a product and skipped the process. The product was never the point.

## The case for the pen

The same logic applies to the humblest tool in the room. In a well-known study, Mueller and Oppenheimer (2014) found that students who took notes by hand did better on conceptual questions than students who typed. Typing encourages copying word for word; handwriting is slower, so it forces you to select, compress and rephrase.

Later studies have complicated this, to be fair. Morehead, Dunlosky and Rawson (2019) confirmed the copying finding but not the performance advantage. A large multi-lab replication (Urry et al., 2021) found no immediate benefit for handwritten notes at all. The honest summary: the effect is smaller, and more dependent on context, than the headlines said.

But the principle survives: shallow processing gives shallow learning, whatever device you hold. And recent neuroscience points the same way. Van der Weel and Van der Meer (2024) used high-density EEG and found that handwriting produces much wider brain connectivity than typing, in regions linked to memory and sensory integration. Forming letters by hand is slow and effortful — exactly the kind of difficulty Bjork had in mind.

## The split I have landed on

So I've settled on a simple division. In the classroom: no AI, no student technology. All writing by hand, in books, in ink. Exams handwritten too — not from nostalgia, but because a handwritten exam is the only format left that shows exactly what a student knows, unassisted, under mild pressure. It's the last honest signal we have.

Homework is where the real battle is. I time assignments so that a suspiciously fast return is itself a flag — a forty-minute essay submitted in twelve minutes tells its own story. Wherever possible, digital submissions go through tools with copy-and-paste disabled. And I tell students plainly: use AI for research if you want — gather, compare, question. But organising your thoughts, structuring your argument, writing the sentences — that has to be your own brain, because that work is the education. The writing isn't the output of the thinking. It is the thinking.

None of this works without vigilance from teachers, and that's the uncomfortable part. AI offers us the same easy life it offers students: let them generate, let it mark, everyone home early. A profession that takes that deal won't notice its own hollowing-out until it's done. Our job, more than ever, is to be the adults who insist on the hard way — kindly, patiently, without exception.

Pen and paper in class. AI, if they must, at home — for research, never for the writing. This isn't a Luddite's charter. It's just taking seriously the oldest finding in the science of learning: the brain keeps what it struggles for.
