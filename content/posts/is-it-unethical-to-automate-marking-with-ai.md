Sunday evening, a stack of essays, a lesson to plan for 8 a.m. — every teacher knows the scene. No wonder AI marking tools are spreading fast. The workload case is genuinely strong. The Department for Education's Teacher Workload Survey (2019) found teachers in England working around 49.5 hours in a typical term-time week, with about one in four over 60 hours, and marking consistently among the heaviest burdens outside the classroom. If a machine can hand several hours a week back to an exhausted profession, only a purist would refuse to look at it.

So let me say the quiet part first: automating parts of marking is not, in itself, unethical. The ethics depend entirely on how you do it.

## The case for caution

Start with what we know about humans and automation. In aviation and medicine, researchers have documented "automation bias" for decades: operators of reliable systems gradually stop checking them. They accept the machine's output even when it's wrong — missing the fault the machine missed, or following the machine into a mistake. A systematic review by Goddard, Roudsari and Wyatt (2012) found the effect in study after study, and the uncomfortable finding is this: expertise is no reliable protection. There's no reason to think teachers with LLMs are exempt. The teacher who lets AI mark a hundred essays will not review a hundred essays. They'll skim ten, find them plausible, and stop. That's not a character flaw. It's how human attention works around competent machines.

The second problem is subtler. Marking isn't just an output; it's how a teacher keeps their grip on the material. When I mark a set of essays, I learn what my class actually understood, where the misconceptions cluster, which of my explanations failed. Outsource that reading and you outsource your own awareness. Teachers who hand their thinking to LLMs for long enough lose their grip on what they teach — and eventually their authority in front of the class. Students can tell, sooner than you'd like, whether the person at the front knows the material.

The third problem is the student receiving it. Feedback is one of the strongest levers in education. Hattie and Timperley (2007) put it among the most powerful influences on achievement, and the EEF's Teaching and Learning Toolkit estimates that good feedback buys around six extra months of progress. But the same research says feedback works when it's specific, timely and trusted — and trust is relational. A generic paragraph from a model that has never met the child is not the same as three precise sentences from a teacher who watched that child wrestle with paragraph two on Thursday. Strip the human out of feedback and school becomes, strictly, less useful. The teacher-student bond, which carries most of the motivation in the whole enterprise, frays.

## A workable ethic

My position is neither refusal nor surrender. It's this: AI may draft; the teacher must decide. Use it for a first pass on routine, low-stakes work — then check every comment before it reaches a student, rewrite what's wrong, personalise what's generic. Keep high-stakes marking — exams, coursework, anything that shapes a child's future — in human hands, with AI at most as a second reader. And never let the tool mark work you haven't read yourself, because reading the work is where your expertise lives.

Put differently: the machine's output should reach the student's desk packaged as human work — checked, edited, and owned by a teacher who could defend every word. That isn't an arbitrary scruple. It's the only arrangement where the classroom stays a connected place: the teacher knows the students' minds, the students trust the feedback, and the saved hours go back into planning and people rather than into more throughput.

Is it unethical to automate marking with AI? No. It's unethical to abdicate it. That distinction is one sentence long, and I suspect the profession will spend the next decade learning to hold it.
