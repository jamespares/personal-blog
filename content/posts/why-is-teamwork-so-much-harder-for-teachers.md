In most workplaces I've known, you can watch your colleagues work. In local government I sat in an open-plan office. At a law firm, junior lawyers learn by sitting in on negotiations. Teaching is different. Once the classroom door closes, you're alone with thirty teenagers, and nobody outside really knows what happens next. It's one of the strangest things about the profession: collaborative on paper, solitary in practice.

The isolation isn't an accident or a personality defect. Judith Warren Little, in research stretching back decades, described the "norms of privacy" that govern teachers' professional relations — a deep, unspoken agreement that what happens in my classroom is my business, and that commenting uninvited on a colleague's teaching borders on rude. Her 1990 essay on the "persistence of privacy" showed how durable this is. Even in schools proud of their collegiality, teachers happily cooperate on logistics — timetables, trips, cover — while keeping their actual teaching politely out of view. Each class is different, each teacher's style is different, and the classroom door does the rest.

The obvious fix is mandated teamwork, and this is where schools often make things worse. Andy Hargreaves coined the indispensable term "contrived collegiality": collaboration imposed from above, timetabled by administrators, outcomes fixed in advance. It produces meetings, minutes and laminated posters about "working together", while quietly increasing managerial control and annoying everyone in the room. Teachers comply, attend, and return to their classrooms unchanged. If you've ever sat through a "collaborative planning session" that could have been an email, you know the feeling.

And yet genuine collaboration clearly matters. Vescio, Ross and Adams reviewed the research on professional learning communities in 2008 and found that well-functioning communities were consistently linked to better teaching and better student outcomes. So we have an apparent paradox: isolation is the default, forced collaboration fails, but real collaboration works. How do we square it?

## Align on outcomes, not on methods

My answer, worked out the hard way in department meetings: stop trying to align methods and start aligning standards. Most teacher teamwork goes wrong because we argue about *how* to teach — whose worksheets, whose starter activities, whose routines. These arguments are unwinnable. Methods are tangled up with personality, and nobody likes being told their style is wrong. Little's research suggests this is exactly where the privacy norm bites hardest.

What we genuinely share is narrower and more defensible: the rubrics and standards we teach towards. A shared essay rubric is an objective thing. Either a student's thesis statement meets criterion B or it doesn't. Two teachers can disagree about every technique under the sun and still mark to the same standard — and if they do, their students get a coherent education whichever classroom they landed in.

Alignment on outcomes then allows honesty about performance. Once we've agreed the objective, we can measure against it fairly, compare notes without defensiveness, and share what we learn. Dashboards help for formative assessment: a simple shared view of quiz scores or rubric progress across parallel classes shows a department where students are struggling, without anyone observing anyone else's lesson. Exam results do the same job at the end of the year. This is Hargreaves' collaborative culture in a workable form — kept alive by teachers because it's visibly useful, built around tasks they actually own, free of the theatre.

The key point: this model respects the privacy norm instead of fighting it. I don't need to watch you teach, and you don't need to watch me. We need to agree what good looks like, measure it honestly, and talk about the gap. Some teachers will hit the standard with discussion-heavy lessons; others with direct instruction and drilling. Both are fine. Forced uniformity of method would buy alignment by destroying the variation that makes different teachers effective with different students — a bad trade.

Teamwork is hard for teachers because the job privatises its most important moments. But privacy in method is compatible with solidarity on standards. Keep the doors closed if you must. Just make sure everyone is teaching towards the same target — and that the numbers on the dashboard are allowed to tell the truth.
